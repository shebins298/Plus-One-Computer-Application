Section 8.1Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a branch of computer science that enables machines to perform tasks which typically require human intelligence. AI is the simulation of human intelligence in machines designed to think, make decisions, predict the future based on patterns in data, and learn and improve through training, feedback or updated data.

AI is used to build agents like robots or virtual assistants that can mimic or simulate human behaviour and make decisions on our behalf.

Features of an artificially intelligent machine

  • Thinks and learns like a human
  • Mimics human intelligence
  • Improves on its own
  • Detects patterns and solves real world problems
  • Comprehends language and images
  • Predicts and takes decisions on its own

8.1.1 History and evolution

PeriodMilestone
1950Alan Turing asks "Can machines think?" and proposes the Turing Test — a human interacts with both a machine and a person via text; if the human cannot tell them apart, the machine passes.
1956Dartmouth Conference — John McCarthy coins the term "Artificial Intelligence", marking the birth of AI as a field of study.
1960s–70sProgress is slow: computers were weak and there was not much data. AI followed strict rules written by programmers and solved only very simple problems.
1980Expert systems mimic human decision-making using rules and knowledge from humans — early systems helped doctors diagnose diseases. MYCIN (1970s) was rule-based.
2000s–nowModern AI — machine learning and deep learning. Systems learn from huge amounts of data; neural networks power face recognition, self-driving cars and recommendations.
Watch out: Alan Turing is credited with the Turing Test. John McCarthy is the one regarded as the father of AI — he named the field.

8.1.2 Applications in daily life

SmartphonesImproving image quality, filtering spam, powering personal assistants
Health careMedical diagnosis, personalised treatment plans, health trackers
TransportationNavigation apps, driver-assistance systems, self-driving vehicles
FinanceFraud detection, personalised financial advice, automated trading
EducationPersonalised learning, automated grading, enhanced education tools

Real-life issues of AI

Asked to generate a recipe for an eggless vegetable omelette, an AI may suggest ingredients that are not easily available, or give steps that are not practical in real kitchens. AI has no human senses like taste or smell, so it cannot judge flavour or texture. Information may also be incomplete or unclear due to limited or biased data.

AI can support humans, but it cannot completely replace human experience and judgment.

Ethical considerations

PrivacyAI collects large amounts of personal data. It must be protected — not misused, shared without permission, or exposed to security risks.
BiasAI decides based on its training data. Biased or unfair data produces unfair or discriminatory results.
AccountabilityIt must be clear who is responsible for an AI decision, especially when mistakes or harm occur.
EmploymentAutomation may replace repetitive jobs. Society must create new opportunities and help people learn new skills.

Section 8.2Machine Learning

Machine Learning is a subset of Artificial Intelligence that enables computers to learn from data without being explicitly programmed. Instead of giving step-by-step instructions, we provide examples, and the machine recognises patterns, draws insights, and makes predictions.

Consider it like teaching a child: you give many examples of apples and oranges rather than describing each fruit in detail. With time the child recognises a new orange or apple on their own — machine learning lets computers "learn" from examples the same way.

8.2.1 Three types of machine learning

SupervisedUnsupervisedReinforcement
DataLabeledUnlabelledNo fixed dataset
GoalLearn input → outputDiscover patterns / clustersMaximise reward
FeedbackCorrect answer suppliedNoneRewards and penalties
ExampleRipe / unripe mangoesClustering customersRobot in a maze
SupervisedTrained on labeled data — each input already has a correct output. Examples: identifying ripe and unripe mangoes; email spam detection.
UnsupervisedWorks with unlabelled data — no predefined answers. Examples: grouping students by learning style; clustering e-commerce customers.
ReinforcementLearns by trial and error with rewards and penalties. Examples: a robot navigating a maze; AI playing chess or Go against itself.

8.2.2 Key concepts

Data QualityData must be accurate, complete and relevant. A face recognition system trained mostly on one ethnicity may fail to recognise people from others.
Training and TestingTraining data teaches the model to learn and identify patterns. Testing data evaluates the model's performance.
Feature SelectionChoosing the most relevant features improves performance and reduces complexity — e.g. predicting student performance from attendance, study hours and previous grades.

8.2.3 Applications

  • Healthcare — predicting disease outbreaks, diagnosing illnesses from medical images
  • Finance — detecting fraud in credit card transactions
  • E-commerce — product recommendation systems like Amazon or Flipkart
  • Education — adaptive learning platforms that adjust lessons to performance
  • Agriculture — monitoring crop health and predicting yield

Section 8.3Artificial Neural Networks

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) are computational models inspired by the human brain. Just as the brain has billions of interconnected neurons that process information, ANNs use artificial neurons — sometimes called nodes — to analyse data, detect patterns and make predictions.

8.3.1 Structure of a neural network

Input LayerThe entry point for data, passing it to subsequent layers. Each neuron represents a specific feature — e.g. each pixel's intensity in image recognition.
Hidden LayersPerform the crucial computations, transforming input data through various functions. There can be multiple hidden layers depending on task complexity.
Output LayerGenerates the final prediction or output, based on the processed information from the hidden layers.
WeightsEach connection has a strength (a weight) that influences how signals propagate — what the network learns and tweaks during training.
The three main layers are input, hidden and output. Weights are not a layer — they sit on the connections between them.

8.3.2 How neural networks learn

  1. Forward Propagation — the input layer sends data to the output layer via the hidden layers.
  2. Error Calculation — the difference between predicted and actual output is measured.
  3. Backpropagation — the network adjusts connection weights to minimise error.
  4. Iteration — the process repeats until the network achieves maximum accuracy.
This is a loop, not a line. Iteration returns to Forward Propagation and repeats until maximum accuracy is reached. Never write it as a dead-end 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 list.

8.3.3 Applications

  • Image recognition — Facebook tagging, Google Photos, autonomous vehicles
  • Speech recognition — converting spoken language into text (Siri, Google Assistant)
  • Medical diagnosis — detecting tumours or abnormalities in X-rays, MRI or CT scans
  • Natural language processing — chatbots, translation tools, sentiment analysis
  • Gaming — AI learns to play complex games like chess or Go
Notes check · 8.3
The input layer, the hidden layers and the output layer.
Through forward propagation, error calculation, backpropagation and iteration. The error between predicted and actual output is measured, weights are adjusted to minimise it, and the process repeats until maximum accuracy is achieved.

Section 8.4Natural Language Processing

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a branch of Artificial Intelligence that enables computers to understand, interpret and generate human language. It helps machines bridge the gap between human communication and computer understanding.

Humans communicate using natural languages such as Malayalam, Hindi and English. In simple terms, NLP enables computers to:

  • Read and understand text, such as emails, messages or documents
  • Listen to and interpret speech, allowing voice-based interaction
  • Translate between languages, helping people communicate across regions
  • Generate meaningful responses, making conversations with machines more natural

8.4.1 The NLP process

  1. Tokenization — breaking text into smaller units like words or sentences
  2. Parsing — analysing the grammatical structure of sentences
  3. Semantic Analysis — understanding the meaning of words and sentences
  4. Response Generation — producing meaningful and context-aware outputs
These four steps are sequential — unlike the neural-network learning cycle in 8.3.2, there is no feedback loop here. NLP combines computer science, linguistics and machine learning.

8.4.2 Applications of NLP

Speech RecognitionTurns spoken words into text — voice-to-text typing, voice commands
Machine TranslationInstant translation between languages — Google Translate
Chatbots and assistantsSimulate human conversation and answer queries automatically — banking bots
Sentiment AnalysisDetects positive, negative or neutral sentiment in posts, reviews or feedback
Content generationAI creating new text — articles, poetry and more

8.4.3 Large Language Models (LLMs)

Modern NLP systems use Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT, which are trained using massive datasets to understand context and generate meaningful responses. NLP is used in spam detection to analyse email content, and Siri, Alexa and Google Assistant use NLP to understand spoken commands.

Notes check · 8.4

Section 8.5Prompt Engineering

Prompt Engineering is the practice of designing clear, precise and effective instructions — called prompts — to get the best responses from AI systems. A prompt is a question or instruction you give to an AI.

The quality of the output largely depends on how well the prompt is formulated. Poorly worded prompts can produce irrelevant or incorrect outputs.

PromptResponse quality
"Write about pollution."Vague, may produce a generic response
"Write five sentences explaining how air pollution affects human health with examples."Specific, detailed, relevant response

Techniques for effective prompting

Be specificPrecisely define the scope and direction of the response. "List three advantages and two disadvantages of AI in education."
Providing contextInclude background, target audience or prior information. "As a teacher, explain how AI can help students improve reading."
Defining output requirementsSpecify word count, bullet points and similar criteria. "Write a 100-word story suitable for class 11 students about robots in space exploration."
Using examplesUseful for complex or creative tasks — demonstrates the desired tone, structure and detail.

Ethical guidelines

  • Do not ask AI to generate personal or sensitive information
  • Avoid spreading misinformation
  • Do not use AI for harmful or dishonest purposes
  • Promote learning, creativity and responsible use of AI

8.5.2 Applications

  • Education — prompts explain concepts, summarise content or create quizzes
  • Content creation — articles, blogs, stories or poetry
  • Programming — generates code snippets or debugging solutions
  • Data analysis — summarises trends or generates visualisations from data

Section 8.6Robotics

Robotics is a branch of science and engineering that combines mechanical design, electronics and Artificial Intelligence to build machines that can sense, decide and act in the real world. A robot is a programmable machine that interacts with its surroundings and acts either fully or partially on its own.

8.6.1 Components of a robot

A typical robot has three main components:

SensorsPerceive the environment — detect light, heat, sound, motion or pressure. E.g. obstacle detection in autonomous vehicles.
ControllersProcess information and make decisions. The "brain" of the robot, using AI or pre-programmed instructions to decide the next action.
ActuatorsPerform movements or actions. E.g. motors, wheels, arms or grippers that carry out the robot's tasks.
Optional components — power supply (battery), communication modules, cameras or additional computing units — are extras, not a fourth main component.

8.6.2 How robots work

Exam note. You are asked to draw this block diagram. Full marks require the feedback arrow from Actuators back to Sensors — without it you have drawn a pipeline, not a control cycle.

Sensing — the robot gathers data from sensors. Processing — the controller interprets data and decides on actions. Acting — actuators execute movements or tasks. Feedback — sensors continuously provide updated information for adjustments.

8.6.3 Applications

  • Manufacturing — assembly line robots increase efficiency and precision
  • Healthcare — surgical robots assist doctors in delicate operations
  • Space exploration — robots like NASA's rovers explore distant planets
  • Disaster rescue — robots enter dangerous zones such as collapsed buildings or nuclear sites
  • Education — robots help students learn coding, AI and automation

8.6.4 Ethical considerations

Employment impactAutomation may replace certain manual jobs
SafetyRobots must be programmed to avoid harming humans
ResponsibilityClear accountability for robot actions must be established
PrivacyRobots with cameras or sensors must respect data privacy

Section 8.7Computer Vision

Computer Vision (CV) is a subfield of Artificial Intelligence that gives computers and other devices the ability to recognise, evaluate and comprehend visual data from the outside world, including pictures and videos — letting machines "see" much like humans do with their eyes.

8.7.1 The computer vision process

  1. Image Acquisition — the computer captures images using cameras or sensors
  2. Pre-processing — cleans the image, removing noise or adjusting brightness
  3. Feature extraction — identifies important characteristics like edges, corners or textures
  4. Recognition and classification — uses these features to identify objects or patterns
  5. Decision making — uses the results to perform tasks like guiding a robot or flagging something
Worked example. A smartphone camera detecting a face to unlock the device: the camera captures the image, identifies facial features, compares them with stored data, and unlocks the phone if they match.

8.7.2 Applications

Medical imagingDetecting tumours, fractures or anomalies in X-rays, MRI and CT scans
Autonomous vehiclesSelf-driving cars detect obstacles, lane markings and traffic signs
Security systemsFace recognition for surveillance and access control
Industrial quality controlInspecting products on production lines for defects
AgricultureMonitoring crops, identifying diseased plants and estimating fruit ripeness

8.7.3 Challenges and ethics

  • Privacy — surveillance cameras and facial recognition may intrude on personal privacy
  • Bias — misidentification may occur if the system is trained on non-diverse datasets
  • Safety — autonomous vehicles rely on accurate computer vision to avoid accidents
Notes check · 8.7
A subfield of AI that gives computers the ability to recognise, evaluate and comprehend visual data such as pictures and videos. It matters because it lets machines "see" and understand their environment, enabling everything from medical imaging to self-driving cars.

Section 8.8AI in Education

Personalised learning means educational tools analyse a student's strengths, weaknesses, learning pace and style, and then suggest exercises, lessons or resources tailored specifically for that student.

Traditional teaching methods often follow a one-size-fits-all approach, but AI enables personalised learning, making education more effective and engaging.

8.8.1 Applications of AI in education

  1. Adaptive learning platforms — analyse performance, adjusting pace and difficulty. Khan Academy suggests lessons based on prior quiz performance.
  2. Digital tutors and chatbots — instant feedback and personalised assistance, guiding students without waiting for teacher intervention.
  3. Learning analytics — tracks progress over time, highlighting areas needing improvement; teachers gain insights for customised strategies.
  4. Automated grading — grades assignments and quizzes, reducing teacher workload.
  5. Inclusive education — accessibility support through voice recognition, text to speech or visual aids for students with disabilities.

8.8.2 The role of teachers

Teachers become facilitators, guiding students through the personalised learning paths AI suggests. They focus on critical thinking, creativity and social skills — areas where human interaction is invaluable.

Teachers remain central to the learning process. AI supports them with insights, saves time on repetitive tasks and offers personalised content, while teachers guide, mentor and motivate students and monitor ethical use of AI tools.

Section 8.9Voice Assistants

Voice assistants are AI programs that comprehend and react to spoken instructions. They play music, set reminders, answer questions and control smart devices by combining speech recognition, Natural Language Processing (NLP) and cloud-based AI services.

They increase accessibility, interactivity and convenience — particularly for those with disabilities or those who would rather speak than type.

8.9.1 How voice assistants work

Section 8.9.1 names four stages — speech recognition, NLP, decision making and response generation. User input and user output are the person, not stages of the assistant's processing.

Worked example

When you say "Hey Siri, what's the weather today?", Siri converts your speech into text, analyses the query to understand you want the weather report, fetches weather data from the internet, and finally responds aloud: "Today's temperature is 28°C."

8.9.2 Applications

  • Smart phones and smart homes — controlling lights, fans, thermostats or security systems using voice
  • Healthcare — assisting elderly or differently-abled individuals to set reminders or call for help
  • Navigation — voice commands in GPS systems to find routes and traffic updates
  • Productivity — setting alarms, reminders, creating calendar events or sending messages
Case study. Alexa, Amazon's voice assistant, allows users to shop online, play music and control smart home devices through voice commands. Over time Alexa learns user preferences, making interactions faster and more personalised.
AdvantagesAccessibility — helps people with disabilities. Convenience — hands-free tasks. Learning support — assists with research and information retrieval.
LimitationsUnderstanding accents — may misinterpret unfamiliar dialects. Privacy — recording and storing voice data. Dependency — over-reliance may reduce critical thinking.
